# Imogen Heap: The Happy Song (2016)

**Sources:** [Goldsmiths press release](https://www.gold.ac.uk/news/sound-of-happy/), [Goldsmiths psychology blog: How to make babies happy](https://sites.gold.ac.uk/psychology/2017/04/24/how-to-make-babies-happy/), [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/surprising-science/scientists-and-imogen-heap-construct-a-perfect-song-for-babies/) (via search snippets), [CNN, 17 Oct 2026](https://edition.cnn.com/2026/10/17/style/happy-song-imogen-heap-anniversary-wellness) (via search snippets). Heap's own page (legacy.imogenheap.com/thehappysong) was down when I checked.
**Confidence:** high on the facts about how it was made and tested. **Low on the actual instrumentation:** none of the sources describe it and I can't listen to the track.

| Element | Notes |
|---|---|
| Genre and era | Playful electronic pop for babies, made for the C&G baby club (agency BETC London, with Felt Music). About 2 minutes. (source) |
| Tempo and key | 163 BPM in 4/4, after testing against 168: the slightly slower version gave parents and babies more time to respond. E flat major, the key Heap's daughter Scout sang in at the first session. (source) |
| Drums and groove | 4/4 was chosen as the easiest to dance and bounce to. Researchers suggested drum rolls to build anticipation. (source) Exact drums: unknown. |
| Bass | Unknown. |
| Samples and harmony | Major key and consonant harmony: babies prefer consonance. Key changes and rising pitch glides were suggested for surprise. (source) |
| Instruments | Not described anywhere I found. Scout's dad whistles. (source) |
| Vocals | Heap sings the lead in an energetic female "motherese" style (sing-song, high energy), ideally recorded with a baby present. Heap's mum sings in the chorus. Scout's giggles and babbles are credited, and the main tune began as her humming. (source) |
| Structure | A simple, repetitive main melody with a chorus. The chorus was the part that worked best. Repetition and interaction were built in so parents join in. (source) |
| Sound effects | Chosen by a parent poll (about 2,300 to 2,500 parents): "Boo!" 66%, raspberries 57%, sneezing 51%, baby laughter 28%, animal noises 23%. A Pomeranian dog is credited. (source) A lyric mentions purring like a cat, but I found no credit for a real cat purr. |
| Mood | Bright, bouncy, affectionate, full of little surprises. |
| Lyrical themes and devices | A journey from the sky to the ocean by bike, submarine and rocket. The transport theme was chosen because it lets the singer use plosive sounds and bouncing actions. Babies responded better to plosives ("pa", "ba") than to soft sounds ("la"). (source) |
| The research | First 4 short tracks (two fast, two slow, each with and without words), tested on 26 babies aged 6 to 12 months. 20 of the 26 preferred the same fast melody. Later rounds tuned the tempo, pitch and plosives. More than 50 babies took part in all. Heart-rate and motion tracking had technical problems. Addyman calls it pilot work: not peer-reviewed, and commercially funded. (source) |

**What Zafar probably likes about it:** it holds his son's attention through constant small surprises (sounds, giggles, plosives) on a steady, bouncy pulse. The problem is that it gets tiring on repeat, so new songs need the same ingredients with a different tune and theme.

**Suno descriptors (no names):** bouncy electronic baby-pop, 163 BPM, bright major key, 4/4, warm sing-song female lead, baby giggles, playful whistling, sneezes, raspberries, "boo!", animal sounds, plosive "ba / pa / boop" syllables, drum-roll build-ups, rising pitch glides, key change into the final chorus, simple repetitive chorus, about 2 minutes.
